Introduction

Criminals have thought up ways, for a variety of reasons, of gaining unauthorised access into just about anything for centuries. This overarching threat is ever-present in a modern world heavily reliant on wireless devices for means of finance, communication, data storage, entertainment and general productivity. However, despite the extensive good these devices have brought and continue to bring society, their greatest strength in being wireless is paradoxically their greatest weakness, with several recent attacks on internet connected devices (IoT devices) demonstrating this, and the potential consequences of these type of attacks resulting in damage to expensive equipment or even loss of life (Papp et al., 2019). The aim of this article is to emphasise the vulnerabilities in both every day and industrial machines and devices, presenting the means of discovering these vulnerabilities, as well as reflecting on two cases of real-world hardware security breaches in the Iranian nuclear centrifuge destroying Stuxnet worm and a very recent, at the time of writing, loophole discovered in modern contactless payments by the University of Surrey in collaboration with the University of Birmingham.

Hardware Hacking: A brief overview

The first step in tricking an electronic device into doing what we want is first understanding how it 'thinks' and how it 'acts'. Deep understanding of a device's inner workings aids in finding potential design flaws that can be exploited to freely alter the device's function, often, but not always, for nefarious means. We get this understanding through the process of hardware inspection which is done in the two to three stages summarised below.

Stage 1: Hardware Reconnaissance

The primary aim of this stage is to obtain as much publicly available information on the target device as possible, making use of the internet as well as model and serial numbers printed on the device to identify datasheets and manuals with valuable information on the device's specifications. It's also important to note that all wireless devices used and or produced in the US have an FCC ID printed on them which can be used on the FCC database site to obtain information surpassing datasheets. For further inspection it's also vital to identify: the power requirements of the device, more specifically, polarity, current and voltage levels, as well as any tamper protection to avoid causing irreparable damage to the device.
